You've opened something a little unusual, so let's start with what it is and what you can do with it — no jargon, no assumptions.

Two things are in front of you.

The first is **TernOO** — a computer, but an unusual one. Most computers work in binary: every switch is on or off, one or zero. TernOO works in *threes* — every position can be negative, zero, or positive. That third value turns out to matter, and you'll feel its effect in small ways all over the place, right down to how the built-in assistant knows when to admit it isn't sure. TernOO's deeper trick is that the pieces of information it handles *describe themselves* — each one knows what it is, instead of relying on some program to remember for it. If that sounds abstract now, it won't for long; it's the single idea everything else grows from, and there's a picture of it in [[ternoo-words|What is a TernOO word]] whenever you're curious.

The second thing is **FlowCode** — the workshop you're looking at right now, the place where you actually build things for TernOO to run. The tabs across the top are all part of FlowCode. Think of it this way: TernOO is the instrument, FlowCode is the studio where you play it.

## The parts you already know, doing more than you'd expect

Here's the part people tend to like. You already know what a flowchart is. You've seen a spreadsheet. You've probably met a drag-and-drop editor for laying out windows and buttons. FlowCode takes those familiar, everyday things — and lets them stop being *descriptions* and start being *the program itself.*

- A **flowchart**, in [[flow|Flow]], stops merely describing what a program should do and actually *does* it.
- A **spreadsheet**, in [[sheet|Sheet]], stops being just a document that adds up numbers and becomes the calculating part of a working program.
- A **layout of windows and buttons**, in [[gui|GUI]], becomes the face a person actually clicks on.

They're **different parts of one program**, each good at something the others aren't. A spreadsheet does maths a flowchart would be clumsy at. A flowchart carries logic a spreadsheet can't express cleanly. A GUI shows and collects what neither of them can. You wire them together — through [[connectors|Connectors]] — into a single working whole. Some programs are just a spreadsheet with a couple of dialogs. Some are a flowchart driving a screen full of controls. Most real ones are a bit of each.

Put together, these familiar pieces are enough to build a genuine, functioning program — and the thing that becomes unnecessary is writing lines of text code. The tools you already half-know, combined, make the code you were dreading redundant.

## A quick tour of the tabs

- [[flow|Flow]] — draw a program as a flowchart. The best place to start.
- [[gui|GUI]] — design the windows, buttons, and dialogs people will see.
- [[sheet|Sheet]] — a spreadsheet whose cells can drive your program.
- [[connectors|Connectors]] — join pieces of a program together by name.
- [[shell|Shell]] — a command line, with the shortcuts you'd expect.
- [[text|Text]] — a text editor that works two ways.
- [[babble-fish|Babble-Fish]] — see your program rewritten in other languages.
- [[academy|Academy]] — meet GHOST, the assistant that lives inside TernOO, and teach it.
- [[mesh|Mesh]] — team up with other machines to share the work.
- [[docs|Documentation]] — you're reading it.

## Where to go next

If you learn by *doing*, go straight to [[first-program|Your first program]] — in a few minutes you'll have built something small and watched it run.

If you learn by *understanding first*, read [[ternoo-words|What is a TernOO word]] — it's the whole idea in one page, with a picture.

And if you're just poking around to see what this is, follow the highlighted links wherever they lead — the pages are threaded together so one idea leads naturally to the next.

One last thing worth knowing up front: nothing here reaches out to the internet on its own. What you build stays yours, on your machine. That's not an afterthought — it's one of the things TernOO is *for*.
