A quick guide

You are not a key. You are you.

Your account is yours alone. No company holds a master key, no one reads your mail, and you can walk away any time and take everything with you. Here is how it works, in plain words.

You never see a key

The hard security math lives inside your phone and does its job quietly, the same way a car key fob just works in your pocket. You open the app, pick a name people will know you by, and use it. There is no key to copy down and no password to invent.

The one idea behind everything here: your gadgets hold the keys, and your account is the steady thing people connect to. Think of a house. The address stays the same. The keys that open it live on your keyring, with a neighbour, in a drawer. Losing a key never moves your house.

Meeting people

You give your contacts the names you choose, like in your phone's address book. The app always shows you your own name for someone, and shows whether it has checked that they are really them.

Verified

Someone you have added. You see your name for them with a check mark. Only a contact you set up yourself earns the check.

Not yet yours

A stranger who says they are "Sam." Shown quietly as a claim, never styled like a real contact, until you choose to add them.

Careful

A new, different key trying to borrow a name you already use. You get a loud warning: this is not your contact.

Four ways to connect, easiest first:

  • Scan a code in person. Two phones on a table. Works with no internet.
  • Get introduced. Someone you both trust taps "introduce you," and the app confirms it really came from them. No public username needed.
  • Send an invite link. For a first contact with no mutual friend, send a one-time link over a channel you already trust, like a text or an email. It works like sending a video-call link.
  • Paste an address. For the technically minded, paste someone's address and confirm a short safety code together.

When you have no friend in common

If you and a stranger share no contact and no earlier channel, there is nothing to check against yet. The app trusts the first connection, remembers it, and lets you upgrade to "verified" later with an introduction or a quick safety-code check. It never pretends to be sure when it is not.

Sending and getting mail

Your message is locked on your own device before it leaves. The server that passes it along only ever sees a scrambled envelope. It cannot read a single word, and neither can anyone who runs it.

No spam, by design

Your mailbox only accepts letters from people you have let in. A stranger cannot drop mail on you, and anyone who turns into a nuisance can be removed once and for good.

Groups

A group is a shared space with its own name, like a family chat or a crew mailbox. Everyone inside sees the same letters. When someone leaves, the group quietly changes its lock so the person who left cannot read anything sent afterwards.

Letters sent before they left were already theirs to read. Changing the lock protects what comes next, which is the part that matters.

If you lose your phone

This is the moment people worry about, so it is built to be calm:

  • Have another device? Tap "I lost a device." The lost phone stops working immediately, and everything carries on where you left off.
  • Lost everything? A few people or backups you chose at the start, your guardians, approve your new phone becoming you. There is a short window to cancel in case it was not you.

No single guardian can do anything, and guardians can never read your messages. Getting back in takes several of them together, so pick people you would trust with that. No company resets your account, because none ever held the keys.

The honest part

Because no company holds a master key, if you lose every device at once and cannot reach your guardians and kept no backup, you can be locked out. That is the price of true ownership. Setting up guardians takes one tap, and keeping a second device makes this almost impossible. It is worth doing on day one.

Your data is yours

You can leave a provider whenever you like and nothing is held hostage. Your letters were never theirs to keep in a form they could read, so moving on is simple.

  • Leaving is just pointing somewhere new. Your account and your contacts come with you.
  • Revoking really works going forward. When you leave, a provider can store no new mail for you and can serve none — you do not have to trust anyone to stop. (Mail you already received was read on your own device; leaving starts a fresh page rather than carrying the old mailbox.)
  • No lock-in, ever. If a service treats you badly, you walk, and that choice keeps everyone honest.

For the curious

Power users can turn on extra safety nets like a written recovery phrase or a hardware key. And if you want total independence, you can run your own server at home from a single card and host everything yourself.
