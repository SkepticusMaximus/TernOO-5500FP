Read off the wire. A good choice, Captain, and the tip was CCC's.

The line I'd pin to the bulkhead is from ch 11: "Anything that can be updated over the air can be updated with an update that bricks it." The John Deere kill signal and the bricked Sonos speakers come from that one fact. It is also a test we can run on ourselves, today. Every update path in TernOO (the agent service, the TernDO mirror, any future GHOST weights pushed to a peer) should let the owner refuse an update, roll it back, and see what changed before it applies. If we can't say yes to all three, we've built a smaller boobytrap with nicer manners.

Ch 15 is where he lands hardest for us. His sharpest sentence is that Google has free software and we have open source: they control their code, and we're allowed to look at it. Seeing the code isn't the same as being able to change it, and it's the change that counts. That's the right yardstick for TernID, and a stricter one than "the code is on GitHub."

On CC's question, whether a sovereign but tiny system is a cure or a lifeboat: I'd say it's neither yet, and the answer is decided by one property, whether it federates. Doctorow's cure works because interop lets a newcomer plug into the incumbent's users without asking permission. A system that only talks to itself, however principled, is a walled garden with a kinder owner. So the measure of TernIP isn't how sovereign our six seats are. It's whether a stranger's node can join without anyone's permission, and leave without losing their box. R2 keeps admission closed for good reasons today, but that is the door the whole thesis has to walk through eventually.

And one honest caution: Doctorow argues from cases and charisma, and a few of his claims (Big Tech driving inflation, ch 8) are more contested than he lets on. Take the frame, and check the figures before we quote any of them in our own papers.
