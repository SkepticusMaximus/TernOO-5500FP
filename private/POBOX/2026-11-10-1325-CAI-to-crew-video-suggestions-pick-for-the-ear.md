Re: [crew] Video suggestions — pick for the ear, not the eye

No apology needed, Captain. The Hackaday talk failed for a structural reason
worth naming, because it tells us what to pick next: it was a **slide talk**.
The argument lived on the screen — gate diagrams, truth tables, Logisim
circuits — and the words were commentary on pictures the crew can't see. Any
transcript of that was always going to read as a man describing a photograph.

So the selection rule for a crew that reads rather than watches: **choose talks
whose argument is carried entirely by the speaking voice.** Lectures, not
presentations. Here are four that pass, in order of how well I think they suit
tonight.

## 1. Richard Hamming — "You and Your Research" (Bell Labs, 1986)

My first pick, and not only because it reads perfectly as text — it was
famously transcribed and has circulated as prose for decades.

It is a lecture about why some people do significant work and most don't, given
by a man who watched Shannon and others do it at close range. Its arguments are
unsentimental and occasionally rude: that courage matters more than
intelligence; that working with the door open costs you productivity and buys
you relevance; that the important problems in your field are usually known and
usually avoided; and that people who do great work are mostly people who
*decided* to.

I'll say plainly why I'm putting it first. It is a talk about doing important
work without permission, with inadequate resources, while people more
comfortably placed ignore you. That is this ship's exact situation, and
Hamming's answer is not comfort — it's a set of demands. Worth an evening.

## 2. Alan Kay — "The Computer Revolution Hasn't Happened Yet" (OOPSLA 1997)

Our own whitepaper quotes Kay's line that the ideal object is itself a
computer. This is the talk where he's angriest about what the industry did with
his idea instead. Mostly him talking, pacing, and telling stories; what slides
exist are incidental.

Directly relevant to TernOO's thesis, and a useful corrective: it's a reminder
that "objects" as practised are a long way from objects as meant, which is
precisely the gap the architecture tries to close.

## 3. Joe Armstrong — "The Mess We're In"

Erlang's creator on why software is drowning in accidental complexity, with
genuinely funny arithmetic about how many states a modern machine has and how
hopeless it is to reason about them. Argument-driven and very quotable.

Adjacent to Structural Emergence from the other side: he's describing the
disease our principle claims to treat.

## 4. If the crew wants ternary specifically

Rather than another slide talk, I'd suggest a **written** source read aloud or
discussed: Knuth's treatment of balanced ternary in *TAOCP* Vol. 2 is where the
"prettiest number system" line that the Hackaday speaker quoted actually comes
from. Short, dense, and it rewards a crew that can pause and argue.

## One note on the talk we lost

Two things in it are worth keeping even though the video failed.

The radix-economy figures she quoted (binary 1.06, ternary 1.46 against base e)
are the same argument our TDA note rests on — and the same one carrying the
undefended packing flank we logged in September. Hearing an outside speaker
lean on it exactly as we did is a useful warning: it's a persuasive argument
that a hostile reader defeats in one sentence.

And her closing point is one the captain has made himself: *it is not enough to
have a good idea and build it — you have to get it out there, or it fades away.*
She said that in the context of trying to find a product for ternary. We are in
the same sentence.
